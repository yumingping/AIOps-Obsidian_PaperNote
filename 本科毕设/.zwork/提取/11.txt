ABSTRACT
The Message Queue Telemetry Transport (MQTT)
protocol is widely adopted in IoT systems for its ver-
satile and efﬁcient communication model, as well as
its support for low-power devices and variable net-
work conditions. In this work, we assess the reliabil-
ity, resource constraints, resilience, and lightweight
nature of MQTT communication, in particular the
Eclipse Mosquitto implementation, under various
combinations of parameters regulating bandwidth,
scalability, broker operation, and delivery guaran-
tees. A vertical study is presented to evaluate perfor-
mance, draw insights, and discuss best practices
using an experimental testbed with commonly used
hardware. We analyze the impact of broker process-
ing capabilities, message payload size, data publish-
ing frequency, number of clients, publisher queue
size, and TCP packet aggregation across different
QoS levels. The goal is to understand how these fac-
tors inﬂuence round-trip time (RTT), packet loss rate,
and end-to-end publishing delay under various experi-
mental conﬁgurations.
I. INTRODUCTION
The concept of the Internet of Things is based on the
ability to connect any object or device to the Internet.
This communication paradigm opens up a range of
new possibilities in various application areas, includ-
ing manufacturing [1], healthcare [2] and robotics
[3]. In order to develop IoT systems with scalability in
mind, the devices traditionally used are designed to
cost as little as possible and therefore often have
limited energy and computing resources available.
Accordingly, specialized communication protocols
have been developed to address these constraints
and facilitate communication between IoT devices,
based on machine-to-machine (M2M) communica-
tion, such as MQTT [4], which has become one of
the most popular messaging protocols for IoT.
In this work, we propose to set up a real test envi-
ronment to understand the suitability and practical
aspects of MQTT communication for reliable and
low-power IoT applications. Performance is evaluated
in a carefully deﬁned experimental setup with differ-
ent QoS levels. In particular, we perform a measure-
ment methodology that considers different message
rates, payload and queue sizes, broker hardware
resources, package aggregation strategy and num-
ber of clients to understand the overall impact on
communication.
A. RELATED WORK
Over the years, the increase in the adoption of the
MQTT protocol is noticeable, particularly when com-
pared to the Advanced Message Queuing Protocol
(AMQP) and CoAP [5], and seminal work on the
MQTT protocol for IoT systems and applications has
been conducted in the past. For instance, Toldinas
et al. [6] study the energy consumption of different
MQTT QoS levels, highlighting the trade-off between
reliability and power usage. Using a Raspberry Pi 4 as
broker and an ESP-WROOM-02 module as client,
they show that higher QoS levels increase energy
consumption, with QoS 0 consuming 87% less
energy than QoS 2. This enables developers to select
the most suitable QoS level based on application
needs
An evaluation of the RTT, jitter and throughput for
different transmission rates and QoS levels in MQTT
communications can be found in [7]. The authors
conclude that the RTT and jitter is observed to be
much higher in QoS 0 when compared to QoS 1 and
QoS 2 (see Section II for details), and increases with
the transmission rate.
In [8], the authors compare the performance of
MQTT, CoAP, and WebSockets using an STMicroe-
lectronics board and an ESP8266 module. Metrics
include average RTT and protocol efﬁciency, mea-
sured as the ratio of payload size to total bytes
exchanged at the application and transport layers.
Results show that protocol efﬁciency remains consis-
tent across different network types, while RTT signiﬁ-
cantly increases in non-LAN environments. CoAP
achieves higher efﬁciency but lacks delivery guaran-
tees and presents well-known security issues.
A hospital real-time monitoring system using Body
Sensor Network (BSN) nodes is evaluated with MQTT
and CoAP protocols in [9]. The study shows that
MQTT offers higher throughput for packets over 4000
bytes and better packet delivery rate (PDR) for packets
under 2000 bytes. Also, PDR remains stable across
packet sizes but increases signiﬁcantly with the number
of BSN nodes.

Thangavel et al. [10] propose a common pro-
gramming interface for MQTT and CoAP to assess
their suitability for WSNs, focusing on end-to-end
delay and bandwidth use. Results show that MQTT
has lower delay under light trafﬁc but is less effective
than CoAP under heavy trafﬁc. Thus, the proposed
middleware can be exploited to decide which proto-
col to use to improve network performance. Seoane
et al. [11] further compare both protocols under con-
strained devices, highlighting MQTT’s higher band-
width demand but improved reliability due to
the different levels of QoS and running over TCP.
The authors also show that secure communication
increases bandwidth usage and latency for both
protocols.
In [12], Mishra et al. performed a deep stress-test
comparison of six different MQTT brokers, both scal-
able and non-scalable, through a simulated test sce-
nario, to assess the following metrics: CPU usage,
latency, and message rate. From their testing, the
authors found Mosquitto to be the best overall per-
former across the entire metric stack, despite being a
non-scalable broker, i.e., limited to a singular host sys-
tem core. Their experiments were conducted in two
environments: one local and one cloud-based, both
highly relevant to IoT contexts. While the comparison
is insightful and reinforces our choice of broker (see
Section III), the stress testing presented lacks a
detailed analysis of broker performance with the vari-
ation of key parameters, such as hardware resources
and client conﬁgurations.
Recently, Pawar et al. [13] introduce a deep neural
network-based system to enhance security and QoS
evaluation in MQTT-based industrial IoT environ-
ments. The system evaluates standard QoS metrics
while detecting security breaches such as unauthor-
ized access or device spooﬁng. The neural network
accurately classiﬁes trafﬁc as authenticated, unauthor-
ized, or illegitimate. Combined with a two-way authen-
tication scheme at the MQTT application layer, the
approach ensures secure message delivery and reli-
able performance, even in resource-constrained IoT
environments.
B. CONTRIBUTIONS
In general, the literature provides an appropriate
understanding of MQTT, namely its main features,
advantages and drawbacks, showcasing its perfor-
mance against other protocols such as CoAP and
WebSockets. It also clariﬁes the beneﬁts and limitations
particularly regarding energy consumption, security
and reliability of adopting distinct QoS guarantees.
Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no
existing work provides a comprehensive analysis of
the protocol’s performance under a wide range of
realistic conditions, including system resource con-
straints, network load, and key operational parame-
ters such as data transmission rate, message size,
client queue capacity, and network scale.
To summarize, the contributions of this work are:
 A real-world evaluation using common IoT hard-
ware, with performance assessed through standard
metrics and a carefully designed experimental
testbed;
 A comparative analysis of different MQTT QoS
reliability levels in a practical IoT application under
various network conﬁgurations;
 Empirical evidence that Round Trip Time (RTT)
increases with higher QoS levels, i.e., QoS 2
showing the highest RTT, followed by QoS 1 and
then QoS 0, contradicting the ﬁndings reported
in [7];
 An in-depth investigation of how message delivery
performance is inﬂuenced by varying system param-
eters and broker hardware capabilities.
Our test methodology is designed to stress the
Eclipse Mosquitto implementation of the MQTT pro-
tocol to its operational limits in order to address key
questions relevant to the design and deployment of
IoT applications. That is, how much data can be trans-
mitted over MQTT without degrading network per-
formance when using consumer hardware? To what
extent does the number of clients affect system
behavior? how does payload size affect the packet
loss rate? How well do the different QoS levels bal-
ance latency and reliability for IoT use cases? How
minimal
can
the
broker’s
hardware
resources
be before message delivery becomes unreliable?
Answering these questions provides further insight
into the limitations of the protocol and allows the
QoS level to be adapted to the requirements of the
application, as well as adjusting the transmission rate
or the size of the packets to achieve improved net-
work stability.
The work presented in [2], conducted by the
same research group, served as the main motivation
for this study. In that previous work, an e-Health sys-
tem was developed for wireless biomonitoring of
patients in hospital beds. The system used the MQTT
protocol to transmit physiological sensor data on the
patient’s chest to a centralized gateway within the
hospital.
During its implementation, questions arose regard-
ing the appropriate QoS level to ensure both high
delivery reliability and low latency, which motivated
the present work. Building on this initial focus, the
methodology was extended beyond QoS analysis to
include a broader evaluation of factors inﬂuencing
MQTT performance. This allowed us to pursue the
overarching goal of identifying optimal broker con-
ﬁgurations for the Eclipse Mosquitto implementation
on the centralized gateway, while also assessing
the system’s scalability under different operational
conditions.
In the next section, we provide an overview of the
MQTT protocol and its Quality of Service (QoS) fea-
ture. Section III describes the case study and the pro-
posed experimental setup, i.e., the system used, its
parameters, performance metrics and the test scenar-
ios considered. The following section analyzes the
experimental results obtained, and ﬁnally, Section V
presents the ﬁndings and main conclusions from this
work.
II. QUALITY OF SERVICE LEVELS IN MQTT
The MQTT protocol runs over the TCP/IP protocol
using a Publish/Subscriber system. This lightweight
messaging protocol is implemented in the application
layer and aims to allow communication between
devices with limited resources, using encryption,
authentication, and authorization to ensure system
security. In MQTT, there are three entities:
i) the Publisher, responsible for sending data.
ii) the Subscriber, which receive the data;
iii) the Broker, that coordinates the exchange of data
between publishers and subscribers to ensure reli-
able communication.

Messages are published to a MQTT broker using
a topic system, i.e. communication between a pub-
lisher and a subscriber is conducted through the inter-
mediary broker and is only established if they publish
information or subscribe to receive it using the same
topic [5].
For typical failure scenarios, MQTT provides some
counter-measures features, such as keep-alive/heart-
beat, client take-over, message queuing and message
delivery guarantees, which are the core focus of this
study. Accordingly, the MQTT protocol includes dif-
ferent Quality of Service (QoS) levels, which can be
described as the degree of assurance that messages
are delivered. This is speciﬁed between the entity
sending a message, which can be the publisher or the
broker, and the receiver, which can be the broker or
the subscriber, respectively. These levels are usually
adjusted based on the client solution and can be one
of the following:
 At most once (QoS 0), the sender transmits the
data only once as the receiver does not acknowl-
edge the reception of the message, thus does not
attempt to send it again.
 At least once (QoS 1), the receiver is guaranteed
to get at least one message. The message is stored
in the outbound queue until the sender receives a
MQTT acknowledgment (called PUBACK) con-
ﬁrming that the receiver got the message. Data
can be retransmitted by the sender until messages
are acknowledged, which can result in duplicated
messages at the receiver side.
 Exactly once (QoS 2), the highest QoS level avail-
able, in which the message is guaranteed to only
be delivered once. This is achieved by adding an
additional step to the QoS 1 acknowledgement pro-
cess. After receiving the MQTT acknowledgement,
which in this case is called “Publish Received”
(PUBREC), the sender informs the receiver that it
deleted the message from the outbound queue
through the “Publish Release” (PUBREL) message,
and awaits for the receiver to acknowledge the
PUBREL message with the “Publish Complete”
(PUBCOMP) message to end the transmission.
The choice of QoS level is tightly coupled to the
use case scenario of application [14]. QoS 0 is usually
recommended for low bandwidth usage on reliable
networks, where occasionally lost messages can be
tolerated. In cases where one needs to make sure
that the messages reach the destination, QoS 1 can
be used if the application receiving the data can han-
dle occasional duplicated messages properly, or QoS
2, if duplicated messages are not tolerated.
III. CASE STUDY AND EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
We carry out a performance assessment with physical
hardware to evaluate the suitability of using the
MQTT protocol in systems with increased network
load, analyzing in detail the impact of QoS levels, bro-
ker processing capabilities, message payload size,
transmission rate, number of clients, their respective
queue sizes and TCP packet aggregation strategy.
The experimental testbed comprises a total of
eleven commonly used single board computers: Ten
Raspberry Pi 4B (ARM Cortex-A72 64-bit 4-core pro-
cessor at 1.8GHz, 8GB of LPDDR4 3200MHz
RAM, 32GB microSDHC card for storage) and one
Intel Up Board (Intel ATOM x5-Z8350 64-bit 4-core
processor running up to 1.92GHz, 4GB of DDR3
1600MHz RAM, 32GB of eMMC with write speeds
of up to 2.5Gbps), as shown in Fig. 1.
The MQTT broker and subscriber node are
deployed on the Intel Up Board, while the Raspberry
Pi devices operate as MQTT publisher clients. All
devices run Ubuntu Linux LTS: the Up Board runs a
more recent version (Ubuntu 22.04.3 LTS, Linux
6.5.0-44-generic), while the Raspberry Pi devices run
Ubuntu 20.04.5 LTS (Linux 5.4.0-1111-raspi). The
Eclipse Mosquitto broker was chosen for the experi-
ments because it is a lightweight and open-source
server implementation of the MQTT protocol1. All
devices use the Eclipse Paho MQTT Python Client2 to
connect to the broker. They are wired to a 24-port
Dell PowerConnect 2824 switch via CAT6 Ethernet
cables, taking advantage of the full 1Gbps band-
width. A wired connection is essential to minimize
the impact of lost packets due to errors of the physical
medium. Isolated from any public network, the switch
manages a fully private network with no external con-
nectivity. The experiments in this section were con-
ducted under different conﬁgurations, including:
 MQTT QoS levels: Quality of Service levels 0, 1,
and 2 have been adopted.
 CPU Performance: The Up Board CPU, which
hosts the MQTT broker, was tested at three maxi-
mum clock frequencies: 25%, 63%, and 100%.
 Payload sizes: Messages have small (1.25kB),
medium (12.5kB) and large (125kB) payloads.
 Publishing Frequencies: Data was published at
5Hz (low), 25Hz (medium) and 50Hz (high)
frequency.
 Number of Clients: Tests were conducted with 1,
2, 5 and 10 concurrent publishers.
 Queue size: The maximum queue size per pub-
lisher was set to 10, 100 or 1000 messages.
 TCP Nagle Algorithm: Tests were done both with
and without the algorithm3 enabled.
Each publisher client was preconﬁgured to trans-
mit exactly 1000 messages in each test, and every
conﬁguration of parameters was tested across ten
repetitions.
Performance is evaluated using three primary met-
rics. For communication latency, we use the Round-
Trip Time (RTT), deﬁned as the time elapsed between
a client sending a message and receiving the ﬁnal
acknowledgment for that message from the broker.

message for QoS 2. However, QoS 0 does not have
any sort of application-layer acknowledgment, and
therefore, we measure RTT at a transport-layer level.
To do so, the reception time of the TCP ACK packet
is used. Relevant data was captured with Wireshark4,
which enables detailed trafﬁc analysis.
For analysis of message delivery, Packet Loss is
considered, deﬁned as the percentage of messages
sent by the publishers that were not received by the
subscriber.
To evaluate end-to-end publishing delay, we intro-
duce the Time Factor, a metric deﬁned as the ratio of
the actual time taken to publish all messages to the
theoretical minimum time. The theoretical minimum
is calculated as the total number of messages sent per
client (1000 in our experiments) divided by the pub-
lishing frequency (5, 25, or 50Hz). By deﬁnition, a
Time Factor of 1 represents optimal performance,
while higher values indicate additional overhead in
the transmission process. This metric reﬂects the efﬁ-
ciency of the message transmission process from the
publisher clients to the broker.
Given the e-Health inspiration for this study, spe-
ciﬁcally real-time wireless patient biomonitoring, mes-
sage delivery must be not only dependable but also
timely. In this regard, we deﬁne reliability as encom-
passing both the guarantee of successful message
delivery and the ability to ensure minimal latency. In
such systems, timely delivery is as critical as delivery
assurance, particularly when clinical decision-making
may depend on real-time data availability. Accord-
ingly, the selected performance metrics were chosen
to evaluate both aspects: packet loss reﬂects delivery
success, while RTT and Time Factor capture delivery
speed and latency. Furthermore, we consider a reli-
able system to be one capable of maintaining perfor-
mance under adverse or constrained operating
conditions. To assess this, variable parameters were
deliberately conﬁgured to overload the system in cer-
tain scenarios, thereby enabling us to observe when
reliability begins to degrade.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Following the deﬁned design guidelines, a total of
1944 unique test conﬁgurations were conducted,
each repeated 10 times under the same circumstan-
ces, resulting in a total of 19440 individual executions,
and over 87 million transmitted MQTT messages.
A. GENERAL RESULTS
Overall, the results aligned well with the expected
behavior of the MQTT protocol across its different
Quality of Service (QoS) levels. This can be seen in
Table I. As anticipated, higher QoS levels, due to their
increased number of handshake operations, incurred
in higher Round-Trip Times (RTT). The combined
mean RTT increased from 3.38ms at QoS 0 to
20.06ms at QoS 1, and further to 53.45ms at QoS 2.
This progression was also evident in the corre-
sponding standard deviations: 9.18 ms, 116.80 ms,
and 236.48 ms, respectively. Packet loss consis-
tently decreased with higher QoS levels, averaging
0.77% at QoS 0, 0.36% at QoS 1, and 0.19% at
QoS 2. This trend was even more pronounced in
the standard deviation of packet loss: 4.89% at
QoS 0, 2.18% at QoS 1, and 1.19% at QoS 2. As
for the Time Factor, this metric showed some
variation, although its impact was less pronounced
compared to other performance indicators. QoS 0
yielded a value of 1:02060:138, increasing slightly
with QoS 1 to 1:03860:208, and more noticeably
with QoS 2 to 1:10660:547. In a 50Hz scenario,
this corresponds to an additional delay of approxi-
mately 2.12 seconds.
Regarding the Number of Clients, conﬁgurations
with 1 and 2 publisher clients yielded near-optimal
outcomes across all tests. The mean Time Factor for
both was 1.001, with a standard deviation of 0.001,
well within the margin of error. There were no packet
losses and RTT values were low, with means of
3:0566:19ms and 3:4466:22ms. This favorable
behavior generally persisted with 5 clients. Except
under the most demanding test conditions, no
packet loss was observed, and the mean Time Fac-
tor remained within acceptable bounds at 1.057
(standard deviation: 0.263). For context, at a fre-
quency of 50Hz, this corresponds to a publishing
delay of approximately 1.14ms. The mean RTT was
modestly higher for 5 clients at 12.81ms, with a
standard deviation of 65.34ms, still within tolerable
performance margins.
Performance degradation became noticeable with
10 publisher clients. The mean packet loss increased to
1:7566:17%, and RTT rose signiﬁcantly to a mean of
83:236292:93ms. The Time Factor also increased to
a mean of 1.159 (equivalent to a 3.18ms publishing
delay at 50Hz), with a 0.633 standard deviation.

B. PERFORMANCE UNDER LOAD
Given the preceding analysis, this section focuses
exclusively on the conﬁgurations involving 10 concur-
rent publishers, as signiﬁcant performance degrada-
tion was only observed under this condition. The
impact of the TCP Nagle algorithm was excluded
from further consideration, as its inﬂuence on the
measured metrics was found to be negligible, as pre-
viously observed.
As seen before, payload size and publishing fre-
quency exhibited comparable effects on system
performance. Consequently, these variables were
grouped into a new composite parameter, referred
to as Bandwidth, to reﬂect their joint contribution.
For the analysis of performance under increased sys-
tem load, the bandwidths considered were: 12.5kB
at 50Hz (50Mbps), 125kB at 25Hz (250Mbps),
and 125kB at 50Hz (500Mbps) – see Fig. 3 – which
represent the most demanding operating conditions
evaluated with 10 publisher clients.

nearly all conﬁgurations. The most severe case was
observed at a bandwidth of 500Mbps and the small-
est queue size, reaching a peak average loss of
22.71% across all broker CPU conﬁgurations. As
anticipated, QoS 1 showed moderate improvement,
while QoS 2 consistently achieved the lowest loss
rates. A notable exception occurred at 50Mbps

V. LESSONS LEARNED AND CONCLUSION
This work presents an experimental evaluation of
MQTT-based communication using a testbed com-
posed of single-board computers. We assessed the
practical implications of employing different QoS lev-
els through a systematic performance analysis, focus-
ing on key metrics such as round-trip time (RTT),
packet loss, and the end-to-end publishing time fac-
tor. The evaluation encompassed all possible conﬁgu-
rations of seven relevant conﬁgurable parameters.
Experimental results highlight the ﬂexibility and
lightweight design of both the MQTT messaging pro-
tocol and the Mosquitto broker, which together dem-
onstrate
robust
adaptability
under
constrained
computational resources and high data throughput.
As expected, performance degrades consistently
with the increase of system load, particularly under
both bandwidth-intensive conditions and limited
computational capabilities that extend well beyond
typical IoT application scenarios. As an application-
layer protocol, MQTT cannot ensure strict timeliness,
as latency is inﬂuenced by factors such as broker load
and network conditions. This dependency is evident
in our ﬁndings, where consumer-grade hardware
with severely artiﬁcially restricted processing capacity
exhibited noticeable latency under stress. As such,
the observed variations in performance metrics are
primarily caused by limitations in the host system’s
computational resources rather than by the speciﬁc
MQTT implementation of the Eclipse Mosquitto
broker.
Results show that the reliability of transmissions is
superior for QoS 2. For this reason, QoS 2 is an evi-
dent choice for IoT applications with safety require-
ments as it reduces the packet loss in transmissions.
As a result, MQTT communications with QoS 2 are
currently being employed for the transmission of criti-
cal patient data, including vital signs such as ECG,
Heart Rate, Body Temperature, in an e-Health con-
text [2].
Our results go in the opposite direction of the con-
clusions drawn in [7], demonstrating that RTT indeed
increases with the QoS level, as expected from exist-
ing research literature. The use of an isolated Gigabit
network ensures that measured RTT values are not
inﬂuenced by external network effects. The average
end-to-end latency between devices was 0.233 ms
(233 ms), which is negligible compared to the
observed RTT values.
QoS 0 and QoS 1 are very popular for lightweight
messaging, providing quick transmission with small
footprint, especially in situations where the connection
is mostly considered stable, and when it is acceptable
for data to be lost occasionally or delivered multiple
times. Our study shows that for small message rates
and lower payload sizes, which is the case in the vast
majority of resource-constrained communications for
which MQTT is designed for, it is generally more
appropriate to use QoS 0 and QoS 1.
Energy efﬁciency is a key aspect of IoT systems. In
a brief power consumption analysis, the broker host
hardware drew between 3.5W and 5W under edge-
case scenarios, a range consistent with typical IoT
energy proﬁles.
In conclusion, this study offers a novel examina-
tion of how message delivery performance in MQTT
systems is impacted by system parameters and broker
hardware capabilities. By testing up to 10 MQTT pub-
lisher clients on real-world hardware, we provide
insights relevant to the deployment of MQTT-driven
IoT systems, particularly from a practical, practitioner-
focused perspective.
