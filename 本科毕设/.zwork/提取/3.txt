ABSTRACT
Publish/subscribe protocols have proven to be a very flexible, effi-
cient, and scalable approach for machine-to-machine (M2M) com-
munication in the Internet of Things (IoT). Due to the convergence
of IoT and industry, and the evolving Industrial Internet of Things
(IIoT), these protocols face new challenges and increasing commu-
nication requirements. However, it is not known to what extent the
protocols can fulfill these requirements. In this paper, we examined
the most commonly used IoT protocols that are either based on pub-
lish/subscribe or define the use of it through standard extensions.
Based on a literature review, we derived concrete requirements
for the use in the industrial domain, compared the characteristics
of the protocols, and evaluated them with regard to these require-
ments. Our results show that none of the examined protocols can
completely fulfill all the requirements and therefore further modifi-
cations are necessary.

INTRODUCTION
The selection of suitable machine-to-machine (M2M) communica-
tion protocols is a crucial task in the design of Internet of Things
(IoT) applications. In contrast to the web, which mainly uses HTTP,
there are hundreds of different protocols in the IoT, each of which
fulfills different requirements [32]. The growing number of de-
vices on the Internet and the associated increase in the degree
of networking has resulted in a need for flexible, efficient, and
scalable solutions. Here, request/response protocols quickly reach
their limits, since they usually work synchronously and not event-
based. Publish/subscribe has proven to be a suitable alternative
for M2M communication. To overcome the problems of request/
response communication, publish/subscribe relies on asynchronous
and event-based communication, in which communication entities
are strictly decoupled from each other [10]. By eliminating direct
point-to-point communication, it enables flexible one-to-many (1:n),
many-to-one (m:1), and many-to-many (m:n) communication. Pro-
tocols such as MQTT and AMQP, which are based on publish/
subscribe, are among the most widely used protocols in the IoT. Due
to the evolution of Industry 4.0 and Industrial Internet of Things
(IIoT), these protocols are also increasingly being used in industrial
environments. However, this shift from the consumer-oriented IoT
to the industrial sector also involves increasing requirements. In
addition to the requirements already known from the IoT, such
as energy efficiency, security, scalability, and interoperability, IIoT
systems must also meet higher communication requirements, such
as real-time capability or high reliability, depending on the critical-
ity of the underlying processes [52]. Protocols that have already
proven their applicability in the IoT do not necessarily have to meet
the requirements in the IIoT. There are protocols, such as OPC UA
and DDS, that have already been developed with a focus on the use
in industrial applications. However, it is not clear to what extent
they can fulfill the requirements.
There are a large number of studies investigating IoT communi-
cation protocols. Many of them are limited to a specific application
area, such as smart grid [55], robotics [28] or wireless communi-
cation [5], and evaluate a certain set of protocols accordingly. We
notice that in most of these works the specific requirements for
the industrial sector are not or only partially taken into account. In
[32], the protocols MQTT, CoAP, AMQP, and HTTP are evaluated
and compared with regard to various requirements. However, some
important aspects such as scalability and real-time capability are

of our findings.

REQUIREMENTS
Since IIoT is a subset of IoT, there are certain overlapping require-
ments [52]. Both have in common that the communication needs
to be interoperable, secure, and scalable in terms of the number
of devices. IoT devices are often battery-powered and wirelessly
connected and thus the focus is on energy and resource efficiency.
The data volume is usually low to medium [52]. Accordingly, the
performance and reliability requirements are lower compared to
the IIoT. Typically, small amounts of data are transmitted with a
low update frequency and packet loss is acceptable. In contrast, in
the IIoT, data volume and data criticality, and consequently also
performance requirements, are high. Industrial applications need
to be highly scalable and often require high reliability, real-time
capability, and low latency. On the basis of the findings in [1], [13],
[31], [52], and [54], as well as our own prioritization, we derive
the five key requirements interoperability (Req-1), security (Req-
2), scalability (Req-3), real-time capability (Req-4), and reliability
(Req-5), which are briefly discussed in the following:
• Interoperability (Req-1) is defined as the ability of two or
more systems or components to exchange information and
to use the information that has been exchanged [18]. Here
we refer to interoperability between applications based on
common standards. A further distinction is made between
technical, syntactical, and semantic interoperability [56]. For
the mere ability to exchange data, it is necessary that the
communication protocols are standardized (technical inter-
operability) and that a uniform data format is used (syntacti-
cal interoperability). In addition, the correct interpretation of
the data is important (semantic interoperability), which must
be ensured on the basis of common data and information
models. To completely fulfill Req-1, each of the three criteria
must be ensured.
• Security (Req-2) needs to be ensured across all layers of
the communication. Therefore, a holistic view of the entire
communication is necessary. However, since the focus is
only on the application layer and transport layer protocols,
only a part of this can be considered here. As a minimum
requirement for the transport layer, the protocol must specify
the use of Transport Layer Security (TLS) in the case of TCP,
or Datagram Transport Layer Security (DTLS) in the case
of UDP to ensure data confidentiality and data integrity.
Otherwise, additional mechanisms must be defined at the
application layer.
• Scalability (Req-3) is characterized as a system’s ability to
manage the increasing number of devices and their informa-
tion [54]. A protocol must scale for hundreds or thousands of
devices (network scalability) and for larger amounts of data
in the range of several megabytes (data volume scalability)
[8] to fulfill Req-3.
• Real-time capability (Req-4) needs to be ensured for mis-
sion- and safety-critical applications to guarantee timely data
acquisition and control [52]. This requires a deterministic
transmission of messages within an exactly defined period
of time. Req-4 is fulfilled if this is ensured for both the ap-
plication layer protocol and the transport layer protocol it
is based on. As with security, it is important that all layers
are considered. Therefore, it is assumed that the underlying
layers are real-time capable as well.
• Reliability (Req-5) of a communication protocol means that
the successful transmission of packets, their correct order,
and the elimination of duplicates are ensured. Therefore, it
is necessary that a reliable transport protocol, such as TCP,
is used. Otherwise, additional mechanisms must be imple-
mented on the application layer. The highest reliability is
achieved by a combination of both transport and application
layer reliability, which is necessary in order to completely
fulfill Req-5.
3
THE IIOT PROTOCOL STACK
Figure 1 shows a non-exhaustive IIoT protocol stack. Publish/sub-
scribe protocols, such as MQTT or AMQP, are implemented on the
application layer on top of a Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)
or User Datagram Protocol (UDP) transport. Security is usually
implemented on the transport layer as well by means of a Trans-
port Layer Security (TLS) or a Datagram Transport Layer Security
(DTLS) binding, respectively. Alternatively, Quick UDP Internet
Connections (QUIC) can be used on the transport layer, which itself
is based on UDP and complements it with additional features. On
the network layer, the Internet Protocol (IP), or more precisely IPv4
or IPv6, is used. Finally, the network interface can be implemented
by means of IP-capable technologies, such as Ethernet (IEEE 802.3),
Wi-Fi (802.11) or mobile communication, such as 5G. This section
provides a more detailed view of the individual layers.
3.1
Application Layer Protocols
Most application layer protocols in the IoT and IIoT domain are ei-
ther based on the request/response or the publish/subscribe pattern.
In this work, we focus on the publish/subscribe protocols. Pure re-
quest/response protocols, such as HTTP, which is one of the most
widely used protocols in the IoT, are therefore not investigated

The publish/subscribe paradigm enables event-based and asynchro-
nous communication between event producing publisher and event
consuming subscriber clients. It achieves a strong decoupling of
these clients in time, space, and synchronization. This means that
they do not need to be available at the same time, they do not
need to know each other (address, port), and they communicate
asynchronously in a non-blocking manner [10]. This results in a
very flexible and scalable approach. However, we do not want to
imply that asynchronous communication and publish/subscribe are
always the most appropriate choice. A very good counterexample
for this is HTTP. The blocking behavior when loading a resource
does not decrease the efficiency and is also easier to implement.
Moreover, the communication can be triggered by the requesting
client, which is not possible for subscribing clients.
From an architectural point of view, a distinction can be made
between centralized and decentralized publish/subscribe systems
[10]. The centralized approach requires a central instance that dis-
tributes the messages between publisher and subscriber. In practice,
the central instance is usually a broker, which is why it is also often
referred to as broker-based approach. Here, the data is distributed
indirectly between publisher and subscriber on behalf of the broker
while the broker maintains a direct connection to publisher and
subscriber. The broker therefore represents a single point of failure
in this approach. In the decentralized or broker-less approach, a
central instance and thus also a single point of failure does not exist,
which is why it is more efficient and scalable. The message distribu-
tion in the decentralized approach is usually realized through (IP)
multicasts [10]. A detailed description and evaluation of publish/
subscribe protocols is given in Section 4.
3.2
Network and Transport Layer Protocols
Since IoT protocols are based on Internet technologies, either IPv4
or IPv6 is used at the network layer. On the transport layer there
are typically the two options of UDP [42] and TCP [43]. In recent
years, the UDP-based protocol QUIC [19] has also become estab-
lished. UDP is a very simple packet-oriented protocol which does
not provide any reliability features. In contrast, TCP is a reliable
connection- and stream-oriented protocol. The use of sequence
numbers, acknowledgements, and checksums guarantees the trans-
mission, order, and deduplication of messages. In addition, TCP
defines mechanisms for flow and congestion control. QUIC aims
to combine the advantages of UDP, being based on it, and TCP to
achieve a lightweight yet reliable communication. It provides mech-
anisms for flow control, low-latency connection setup, network
path migration, security, loss detection, and congestion control
[19]. Although it is used successfully in HTTP/3 [2], it plays rather
a minor role for the IIoT protocols surveyed in this paper. In sum-
mary, protocols based on TCP or QUIC fulfill the requirement of
a reliable transport layer (Req-5), whereas UDP-based protocols
do not. From [8] we can further derive that TCP and QUIC are
not real-time capable due to unpredictable behavior of flow and
congestion control mechanisms. To put this statement in context,
we would like to note that we are referring here only to the un-
predictability of the mechanisms as described in the standards. We
do not categorically disclaim real-time capability through further
modification or replacement of these mechanisms. UDP, in contrast,
is potentially real-time capable if it is supplemented with real-time
capable technologies and mechanisms on the underlying layers and
on the application layer.
3.3
Network Interface
Since data link and physical layer are very closely related and are
specified together in most standards, we group them together as
network interface. In order to meet the high communication re-
quirements, wired communication technologies are often preferred
over wireless technologies. In the past, proprietary bus systems and
Industrial Ethernet (IE) technologies were used almost exclusively
for this purpose. However, the trend is increasingly towards open
standards built on top of standard Ethernet, such as Time Sensitive
Networking (TSN) and Software Defined Networking (SDN). In
addition, the concepts of these technologies are also finding their
way into the new generation of mobile communications (5G) and
Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11ax). However, as already pointed out before, the
consideration of network technologies is beyond the focus of this
work. Therefore, a real-time capable technology is assumed when
evaluating the real-time capability of transport and application
layer protocols.

4.1
MQTT
The Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) is a lightweight
machine-to-machine (M2M) protocol, which implements broker-
based publish/subscribe. It is standardized by the Organization for
the Advancement of Structured Information Standards (OASIS)
and available as versions 3.11 [33] and 5.0 [34]. MQTT is a data-
agnostic protocol and therefore does not define data format or data
modelling capabilities. Due to this characteristic it is possible to
define both according to the specific use-case. Therefore, Req-1 is
only partially fulfilled. MQTT builds on TCP at transport layer,
enabling the use of TLS to secure the communication. In addition,
the standard defines mechanisms for user and device authentication,
authorization of server resource access as well as integrity and
privacy of control packets and contained data [33]. Overall, MQTT
fulfills Req-2. Studies with various broker implementations show
that MQTT scales very well in terms of the number of devices
[27] and can handle up to thousands of concurrent devices. For the
HiveMQ1 broker and with the help of clustering methods, scalability
could be shown for millions of devices [16]. The payload length
of an MQTT packet is limited to 256 MB. Thus, MQTT fulfills
Req-3 both in terms of devices and in terms of data volume. Due
to low complexity and binary encoding, the protocol itself is very
lightweight and potentially real-time capable. However, being based
on TCP, MQTT cannot fulfill Req-4. In contrast, this is an advantage
in terms of reliability. MQTT defines three levels of Quality of
Service (QoS), which allow messages to be sent at most once (QoS-
0), at least once (QoS-1) or exactly once (QoS-2). Through this and
the use of TCP, MQTT fulfills Req-5.
4.2
CoAP
The Constrained Application Protocol (CoAP) is a lightweight
RESTful protocol, which is designed for the use in resource con-
strained environments. It is specified as RFC 7252 [50]. In contrast
to MQTT, the base standard of CoAP describes request/response

communication and only supports publish/subscribe communica-
tion via standard extensions. The CoAP Observe extension [15]
provides a broker-less publish/subscribe mechanism, which en-
ables observation of CoAP resources and notification of subscribers
upon resource state changes. Additionally, a broker-based publish/
subscribe mechanism for CoAP is defined in [26], which is currently
in draft status. Since all parts of CoAP are available as RFC specifica-
tions, CoAP fulfills the requirement of being technical interoperable.
CoAP defines several media types and can make use of Sensor Mea-
surement Lists (SenML) [20] in combination with JavaScript Object
Notation (JSON), Concise Binary Object Representation (CBOR),
Extensible Markup Language (XML), and Efficient XML Interchange
(EXI) and a common data model for sensors. However, this model
is not very mature compared to the data modeling capabilities of
OPC UA, which is why Req-1 is only partly fulfilled. CoAP is based
on UDP and thus can make use of DTLS. Furthermore, with Object
Security for Constrained RESTful Environments (OSCORE) [49]
and Authentication and Authorization for Constrained Environ-
ments (ACE) [40], there are additional mechanisms for ensuring
security at the application layer. Overall, CoAP fulfills Req-2. Due
to its low complexity and as pointed out in [8], CoAP is scalable in
terms of the number of devices. The block-wise transfer option [3]
can be used to send larger amounts of data over UDP. Hence, CoAP
scales both in terms of the number of devices and in terms of data
volume and completely fulfills Req-3. With regard to real-time capa-
bility, CoAP being based on UDP, is potentially real-time capable if
complemented with a time-slot mechanism on top of the transport
layer. As shown in [23–25], it is possible to implement an appli-
cation layer Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) mechanism
to achieve real-time communication. However, this needs also to
be applied to the publish/subscribe extensions of CoAP. Therefore,
CoAP only partially fulfills Req-4. To compensate the unreliable
UDP transport, the CoAP reliability layer defines message IDs and
reliable and unreliable messages, which guarantee message receipt,
deduplication and message order. Altogether, Req-5 is only partially
fulfilled.

4.3
AMQP
The Advanced Message Queuing Protocol (AMQP), which is stan-
dardized by OASIS [14], is another lightweight M2M protocol.
AMQP supports both request/response and publish/subscribe com-
munication. Besides binary encoding, AMQP supports XML and
JSON and has an own type system, which is, compared to OPC UA
for example, not as powerful. Thus, AMQP only partially fulfills
Req-1 and needs further extensions. AMQP is based on TCP, and
hence, can make use of TLS. Additionally, Simple Authentication
and Security Layer (SASL) [60], which provides additional means
for application layer encryption and authentication, can be used. As
a result, AMQP fulfills Req-2. Due to its simplicity AMQP can scale
for a large number of devices. As pointed out in [29], scalability
can further be improved by using a clustering approach, which
eliminates the broker being a single point of failure. Since AMQP
uses TCP as transport, it is theoretically well scalable in terms of
data volumes. As pointed out in [32], the actual message size limit
is a matter of the specific broker implementation. Overall, however,
AMQP fulfills Req-3. Similar to MQTT, AMQP is a very lightweight
and efficient protocol, however, being based on TCP, it is not real-
time capable and thus cannot fulfill Req-4. In terms of reliability this
is an advantage. Being based on TCP and through the definition of
at-least-once message transmission guarantee, AMQP fulfills Req-5.
4.4
XMPP
The eXtensible Messaging and Presence Protocol (XMPP) is an In-
ternet protocol that was initially developed for instant messaging
systems rather than for M2M communication [57]. It is used to
exchange XML data based on a client/server architecture. The core
of specification in RFC 6120 only defines a request/response pattern.
Publish/subscribe communication, however, is available through
the standard extension XEP-0060 [30]. This and other extensions,
such as for service discovery, make it better suitable for M2M com-
munication. The extensibility itself is a major advantage of the
protocol. XMPP does not define its own data modelling capabilities.
Therefore, XMPP only ensures technical and syntactical interoper-
ability and thus only partially fulfills Req-1. In terms of security, the
core specification defines the use of TLS, which is further described
in RFC 7590 [48], and SASL. Additional mechanisms for end-to-
end signing and object encryption are described in RFC 3923 [47].
Hence, XMPP fulfills Req-2. Due to its decentralized architecture,
XMPP is scalable in terms of the number of devices. To send larger
amounts of data, there are different options. In order to achieve
data volume scalability in the range of megabytes, for example, the
in-band bytestream extension can be used, which is specified in
XEP-0047 [21] and allows to send data in smaller chunks. Overall,
XMPP fulfills Req-3. As indicated before, the initial focus on instant
messaging comes with some drawbacks regarding M2M communi-
cation. The authors in [13] point out that XMPP has a rather low
performance due to its high overhead and is thus not real-time ca-
pable in the sense of M2M communication. In [55] additionally the
parsing of XML and the high complexity of XMPP are mentioned,
which make time-critical applications impossible. Finally, XMPP
is based on TCP and thus does not fulfill Req-4 altogether. The
XMPP specification does not provide any mechanisms to guarantee
message delivery, besides being based on TCP on transport layer.
Thus, Req-5 is only partly fulfilled.
4.5
MQTT-SN
MQTT for Sensor Networks (MQTT-SN) is a very lightweight M2M
and publish/subscribe protocol for the use in wireless sensor net-
works. The current protocol specification version 1.2 was developed
and published by IBM. There is an effort to standardize MQTT-SN
as OASIS standard, which is currently in progress. As described in
[53], MQTT-SN was developed with the aim to be a lightweight ver-
sion of MQTT with the focus on wireless sensor networks, which
is agnostic to the underlying layers. One fundamental difference is
that MQTT-SN devices must be connected to an MQTT broker via
a gateway, which is implemented either as standalone gateway or
as integrated gateway in the MQTT broker. Therefore, MQTT-SN
cannot operate independently from MQTT. MQTT-SN is also a
data-agnostic protocol. Thus, it can also only partly fulfill Req-1.
Although being agnostic to the underlying transport and network-
ing technologies, most implementations are built on top of UDP/IP,
and hence, can make use of DTLS. Detailed guidelines on security
and additional mechanisms are missing in the current specification,
which is why MQTT-SN can only partly fulfill Req-2. However, this
is likely to be an issue in the current standardization as security is
a key requirement for the acceptance of a standard. Since MQTT-
SN works the same way as MQTT from a protocol perspective, it
should also scale as well as MQTT does. However, a problem arises
due to the gateway architecture. A standalone gateway, which has
to maintain one or multiple TCP connections to the broker, creates
another single point of failure. A distinction must also be made
between a transparent and an aggregating gateway. A transparent
gateway establishes one connection per device, while an aggregat-
ing gateway establishes exactly one connection for all devices. As
stated in [17], a transparent gateway, which is easier to implement,
scales worse than an aggregating gateway, since a very high num-
ber of devices also requires a very high number of parallel TCP
connections to be kept open. The maximum encodable payload size
is 65,535 octets. As MQTT-SN does not support fragmentation and
reassembly, the actual value is limited by the underlying technology
[53]. Thus, MQTT-SN can only partly fulfill Req-3. Since MQTT-SN
is an extremely lightweight protocol and is also based on UDP, it is
in principle real-time capable. However, as indicated in [8], it has
to be complemented by time-slot mechanism, like TDMA. Req-4
is therefore only partially fulfilled, but can be fulfilled by further
extension. To compensate the unreliable transport, there are QoS
levels, as with MQTT. In addition to the three already described
QoS levels, there is a fourth one, which allows publishing without
connection setup and without any guarantee on message delivery.
Thus, it does not provide further reliability. Overall, Req-5, is only
partly fulfilled.
4.6
OPC UA
The Open Platform Communications Unified Architecture (OPC
UA) is an industrial standard that defines an architecture for inter-
operable and secure communication, which is driven by the OPC
Foundation and published as standard series IEC 62541. It is com-
posed of currently over 20 specification parts. The base standard

described in Part 1 [38] specifies request/response communication
based on a client/server architecture. It defines a basic publish/
subscribe mechanism on the basis of a subscribe/notify pattern.
Part 14 [39] of the standard extends the basic specification with
broker-less and broker-based transport mechanisms. For the broker-
based approach, the standard defines AMQP and MQTT as possible
options. The applicability of an MQTT protocol binding for OPC UA
was demonstrated in [45], for example. It is also conceivable that
OPC UA will be expanded in the future to include other protocol
bindings, such as MQTT-SN or CoAP. The feasibility of integrat-
ing CoAP into OPC UA based on the request/response pattern has
been shown in [58]. Since we have already discussed the individual
protocols, we will concentrate on the broker-less transport using
OPC UA UDP or OPC UA Ethernet. In this approach, messages are
distributed to subscriber based on multicast groups. The UA Data-
gram Protocol (UADP), which is based on OPC UA binary, is used
for data encoding. On top of that, OPC UA defines a very powerful
data and information model that can be extended with regard to
organizational and vendor-specific requirements, which is unique
for all the surveyed protocols. In addition, the standard is driven
by a strong international and industrial consortium. Consequently,
OPC UA fully complies with Req-1. OPC UA provides a rich set of
security mechanisms and policies and was developed with security
in mind. Part 2 of the specification describes mechanisms for au-
thentication, authorization, and encryption. A role-based security
concept is defined in Part 18. In addition, the standard has been
classified by the German Federal Office for Information Security
(BSI) as fundamentally secure [11]. However, this does not apply
or only partially applies to part 14. First of all, being based on mul-
ticast, the use of DTLS is not possible, which is why securing the
transport layer is also not possible. On application layer, end-to-end
security is ensured based on signing and encryption of messages
with compliance to Part 2. Thus, publish/subscribe based on OPC
UA UDP does not completely fulfill Req-2. Therefore, security is an
open research issue for this approach. The use of multicasts makes
the system very scalable in terms of the number of devices due to
its simplicity and the absence of a single point of failure in the form
of a broker. However, we have found that there is a lack of practical
investigations on this topic. Moreover, the use of multicasts and
UDP is a limitation in terms of Internet-wide communication and
data volume scalability. The latter we have already pointed out for
MQTT-SN. Thus, also Req-3 can only partially be fulfilled. In con-
trast, the approach is real-time capable and thus fulfills Req-4. This
could be shown exemplarily in [41] on the basis of the open625412
reference implementation and TSN. For future research, the config-
uration of TSN via OPC UA seems also to be promising. As stated in
[39], with OPC UA UDP there is no guarantee of timeliness, deliv-
ery, ordering, or duplicate protection. However, there are sequence
numbers and retransmission mechanisms to compensate for this.
This means that Req-5 is only incompletely fulfilled.
4.7
DDS
The Data Distribution Service (DDS) [36] is a standard that is de-
veloped by the Object Management Group (OMG). The base stan-
dard specifies a publish/subscribe model, which does not describe
2http://www.open62541.org
the actual communication protocol for message exchange. Thus,
to ensure interoperability between different implementations, the
Real-time Publish-Subscribe Protocol DDS Interoperability Wire
Protocol (DDSI-RTPS) specification was developed, which is based
on UDP transport using IP multicasts. DDS provides an own XML-
based type system to ensure syntactical interoperability. However,
with regard to semantic and organizational interoperability, DDS is
not comparable with the capabilities of OPC UA. Thus, DDS only
partially fulfills Req-1. As with OPC UA, UDP transport over IP mul-
ticasts prevents the use of DTLS. To compensate for this, there is a
separate specification [37] that allows various security mechanisms
to be included as application layer plugins. These include authenti-
cation, authorization, encryption, signing, logging and data tagging.
However, due to the lack of transport layer security, DDS fulfills
Req-2 only partially. As stated in [51], DDS provides a low-latency
data connectivity, extreme reliability, and a scalable architecture for
mission critical applications. Due to its decentralized approach and
being based on multicasts, DDS is highly scalable with regard to the
number of devices. However, as pointed out in [7], the latter is also
an obstacle in scalability of point-to-point scenarios and Internet-
wide deployments where multicasts are not applicable. Although
it might not seem to be as important in the IIoT as compared to
the IoT, it is a limiting factor. It is noticeable that, as with OPC UA,
there are few practical evaluations of scalability in the literature.
In terms of data volume scalability, DDS is also limited due to UDP
on transport. In order to address this limitation, DDS specifies a
mechanism for data fragmentation. Overall, DDS fulfills Req-3 only
partly. DDSI-RTPS was developed with the focus on real-time ca-
pability, which is achieved through the use of UDP and multicasts.
In addition, the use of DDS and TSN (DDS-TSN) is currently being
standardized by OMG. Overall, DDS fulfills Req-4. To counteract un-
reliable UDP transport, the standard defines unreliable and reliable
message transmission. Reliable transmission guarantees message
transmission and message order. Altogether, Req-5 is only partially
fulfilled.
4.8
DPWS
The Devices Profile for Web Services (DPWS) is an M2M protocol
which implements the Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) para-
digm. It was designed to enable communication between resource
constrained devices based on web services. DPWS is standardized
by OASIS in [35]. The standard defines mechanisms for secure web
service communication, web service discovery and web service
description. Publish/subscribe communication is possible through
a broker-less eventing mechanism based on a subscribe/notify pat-
tern similar to CoAP Observe. DPWS is not as common in the IoT
and IIoT as the previously described protocols. However, it has been
successfully used in the medical domain and been further devel-
oped as Medical DPWS (MDPWS), which is standardized by the
IEEE [22]. DPWS uses the Simple Object Access Protocol (SOAP)
for exchanging XML-based structured data. On top of that, there is
no data modeling capability to achieve semantic interoperability.
Thus, interoperability (Req-1) can only be ensured in terms of tech-
nical and syntactical interoperability. The standard provides a set
of mechanisms to achieve secure communication, including TLS,
message signing and authentication. Therefore, it fulfills Req-2. The

CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK
In this survey, we investigated commonly used publish/subscribe
protocols from the IoT and IIoT domain. For this purpose, we con-
ducted a literature research and performed an evaluation with
regard to the requirements interoperability, security, scalability,
real-time capability, and reliability. The evaluation results are sum-
marized in Table 2. None of the investigated protocols could fulfill
all requirements. However, this is in line with our expectations, as
it is obvious that a trade-off between the individual requirements
has to be made in many cases. The fulfillment of the requirements
is often closely related to the choice of the transport mechanism
on which the protocol is based on. TCP is necessary, for example,
to ensure reliable transport, but it is not real-time capable. In con-
trast, the UDP-based protocols are potentially real-time capable.
However, they must implement additional mechanisms to ensure
reliability on the application layer. Only OPC UA and DDS were de-
veloped with real-time capability in mind. However, being based on
multicasts, they lack the possibility of a secured transport layer and
do not scale with regard to data volume. Due to its strong data mod-
elling capabilities, OPC UA can completely fulfill the requirements
for technical, synctactical, and semantic interoperability, which is
unique for the investigated protocols.
Since the evaluation has shown that the protocols do not ade-
quately meet the requirements, new research issues arise. Besides
OPC UA and DDS, which are already developed with the focus on
real-time, also the real-time capability of MQTT-SN and CoAP ap-
pears to be an interesting challenge. For this purpose, the protocols
must be extended to include additional real-time capable mecha-
nisms. In this context, investigating synergies with technologies
such as TSN and SDN also seems very promising. To take advan-
tage of the strong data modeling capabilities of OPC UA, it may
be useful to integrate other protocols into OPC UA, as is already
specified for MQTT and AMQP by the standard. From the evalua-
tion process we can further conclude that in many cases there is a
lack of practical performance investigations in real-world setups.
This applies for example to scalability, particularly for the proto-
cols based on multicast such as OPC UA and DDS, and should be
addressed in future work. In addition, the performance evaluation
based on other parameters, such as throughput, round-trip time
and latency, is necessary to further assess the suitability of the
protocols in industrial applications. Another open issue we want
to address in future work is the integration of QUIC, as it is a very
promising alternative to the established standards TCP and UDP.
Further, as already mentioned, it is not yet considered in the current
specifications of the protocols.
